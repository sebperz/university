### Metabolic engineering of Yarrowia lipolytica targeting bottlenecks to boost D-Pantothenic acid biosynthesis

Xing-Kai Li, Nuo Zhang, Hai-Peng Li, Zheng-Yu Huang, Gao-Yue Niu, Chen-Yi Sun and Jian-He Xu
*Bioresources and Bioprocessing* (2026) 13:15

**Introduction**

D-Pantothenic acid (DPA), also known as vitamin B5, is a water-soluble organic acid, which plays an important role in the biosynthesis of coenzyme A (CoA) and the acyl carrier protein (ACP), as well as in the metabolism of essential nutrients including proteins, fats, and carbohydrates (Barrit et al. 2024; Eggersdorfer et al. 2012; Miallot et al. 2023). DPA has also found wide applications in various industries, including foods (Revuelta et al. 2016), feeds (Zehra and Khan 2018), cosmetics (Pastor-Nieto et al. 2021), and medicines (Xu et al. 2020). Currently, DPA production primarily depends on chemical synthesis. However, it is environmentally unfriendly because of large quantities of toxic gases and wastewater involved in the production process (Rowicki et al. 2006). Microbial fermentation shows a great potential in the sustainable production of natural products, since it can utilize renewable bioresources (Francois et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2023; Acevedo-Rocha et al. 2019). Hence, the construction of a robust cell factory for DPA biosynthesis becomes a hot topic.

In recent years, many researchers have established DPA cell factories using various microorganisms. Among these cell factories, some of researchers chose prokaryotic hosts such as *Escherichia coli* (Zhang et al. 2019; Zhao et al. 2025a), *Bacillus subtilis* (Yuan et al. 2023; Mao et al. 2024), *Bacillus megaterium* (Tadi et al. 2022), and *Corynebacterium glutamicum* (Hüser et al. 2005; Su et al. 2023). In particular, *E. coli* has demonstrated a strong potential for DPA production, achieving the highest DPA titer of 148 g/L with β-Ala supplementation and 65.1 g/L without β-Ala supplementation (Zhang et al. 2025a). A series of metabolic engineering strategies have been adopted to enhance DPA production in *E. coli*, such as competitive pathway inhibition (Zhang et al. 2019), key enzyme modification (Cai et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025a; Qiu et al. 2025), transcriptomic analysis (Song et al. 2024), cofactor engineering (Zou et al. 2022; Qiu et al. 2025), and fermentation optimization (Zou et al. 2021).

However, advances in synthetic biology have facilitated the production of diverse natural products in eukaryotic hosts, particularly in yeasts (Zhang et al. 2025b; Xu et al. 2023). To date, biosynthesis of DPA has been achieved in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (Guo et al. 2023), and a DPA-producing strain of *S. cerevisiae* was successfully constructed by screening seven key genes derived from various species and optimizing the copy number of pathway modules, ultimately reaching a titer of 4.1 g/L in a 1-L bioreactor.

*Yarrowia lipolytica*, an unconventional yeast, has produced a range of organic acids such as succinic acid (Zhong et al. 2024), L-malic acid (Wang et al. 2024), itaconic acid (Fu et al. 2024), and chlorogenic acid (He et al. 2025), demonstrating an enormous potential for producing organic acids. However, there has been no report of attempts to engineer *Y. lipolytica* to synthesize DPA from simple carbon sources. Therefore, we investigated the potential bottlenecks for DPA synthesis in *Y. lipolytica* to progressively enhance the DPA production (Fig. 1).

Herein, we initiated our research from an engineered *Y. lipolytica* strain, Po1f-HHR (Xu et al. 2024), with a significantly improved homologous recombination efficiency, aiming at targeting and breaking its bottlenecks to boost DPA biosynthesis through systematic metabolic engineering approaches. First, identification of rate-limiting steps and combination of key enzymes together achieved synergistic optimization to enhance the DPA titer. Then, weakening of the strongly competitive pathways redirected the metabolic flux for further enhancement of DPA synthesis. By enhancing the multiple cofactor supplies, we improved the efficiency of entire DPA synthesis pathway, achieving a DPA titer of 192 mg/L in shake-flask cultures. Ultimately, the best strain DPA34, achieved a DPA titer of 2.18 g/L through fed-batch fermentation in a 5-L bioreactor, representing the first report of DPA production in the *Y. lipolytica* cell factory.

### Current status, trend and progress of stem cells in tracheal tissue engineering research: an overview and perspectives

Jianwei Zhu, Min Bao, Yibo Shan, Yi Lu, Zhiming Shen, Wenxuan Chen, Hao Chen, Lintao Guo and Hongcan Shi
*Stem Cell Research & Therapy* (2026) 17:18

**Background**

Presently, clinical tracheal reconstruction is a difficult task to tackle around the world. For long tracheal defects (50% or greater in adults or 30% or more in children) due to tumor invasion, tracheal trauma, tracheomalacia, infection, etc., replacement of the trachea is advised [1–3]. The ideal trachea replacement, with suitable biomechanical properties, excellent biocompatibility, no immunogenicity, and no inflammatory response, is urgently needed to reconstruct the trachea both anatomically and functionally [4–6].

Over the last few years, tissue engineering has seen tremendous growth, leading to positive results in areas such as trachea reconstruction [3, 7–10], nerve regeneration [11–14], vascular reconstruction [15–18], cartilage repair [19–22], and bone reconstruction [23–26], and hinting at its boundless potential. Seed cells, as one of the three elements of tracheal tissue engineering (TTE), are a critical factor in the successful fabrication of a tissue-engineered trachea [10, 27]. Stem cells have been widely praised by TTE researchers due to their multifaceted differentiation potential, immunomodulatory properties, and paracrine features [28, 29]. In recent years, with the continuous advancement of stem cell research, numerous scientists have employed stem cells in research related to TTE. By utilizing various types of stem cells, such as mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and pluripotent stem cells, as well as stem cell-derived exosomes, researchers have developed a variety of tissue-engineered trachea grafts that have demonstrated positive effects and have the great potential to be ideal tracheal substitutes [30–34].

Bibliometric analysis is an effective method for qualitative and quantitative analysis of the impact of publications in the field of science, which can be conducted to investigate the research status, trends, and emerging hotpots through dynamic analysis of publications spatially and temporally [35–37]. In addition, it can also extract the authors, institutions, countries, and other information of the publications to objectively reveal the cooperative relationship between the authors, institutions, and countries, and evaluate the academic contribution of them in the research field [38, 39].

This review has carried out a comprehensive overview of stem cells in TTE. In this review, we investigate the research trend, research status, and current research hotspots of the field of stem cell research in TTE based on bibliometric analysis of relevant publications, as well as uncover the major influential countries, institutions, and authors in the research field. Furthermore, a summary of the current stem cell sources in TTE research is provided, along with an exploration of methods for constructing tissue-engineered tracheae based on stem cells and the challenges confronting this field.

### Enzyme-constrained genome-scale model of Yarrowia lipolytica predicts growth-phase specific metabolic engineering targets

Juliano Sabedotti De Biaggi, Young-Kyoung Park, Eduard J. Kerkhoven, Rodrigo Ledesma-Amaro, Petri-Jaan Lahtvee
*Applied Microbiology and Biotechnology* (2026) 110:123

**Introduction**

*Yarrowia lipolytica* is arguably the most consolidated oleaginous yeast for bioprocessing. This yeast naturally produces lipids, organic acids, and polyols. For instance, wild-type *Y. lipolytica* strains have been shown to accumulate lipid contents of 78% (Tomás-Pejó et al. 2023), produce 107 g·L⁻¹ of citrate (Rymowicz et al. 2010), and yield 199 g·L⁻¹ of erythritol (Rakicka et al. 2017). Notably, this reported lipid accumulation is compatible with economically viable key performance indicators (Kumar et al. 2023). *Y. lipolytica* also holds Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) status (Groenewald et al. 2014), which expands its applicability to a broader range of products, including those intended for human consumption. A recent study identified companies that have established or are scaling up commercial *Y. lipolytica*-derived products (Park and Ledesma-Amaro 2023), indicating that the industrial use of this yeast is already a reality. A common feature among most of these products (i.e., polyunsaturated fatty acids, recombinant enzymes, and carotenoids) is their derivation from genetically modified *Y. lipolytica*. This is facilitated by the ready availability of metabolic engineering tools for this yeast (Cao et al. 2023), potentially enabling multiple cycles of targeted genomic engineering.

The availability of metabolic engineering tools makes *Y. lipolytica* a suitable platform for strain development in Design-Build-Test-Learn (DBTL) cycles —an engineering approach to achieve optimal solutions. Implementing this approach requires standardized and modular DNA parts and robust genome-editing capabilities (Chao et al. 2017), both of which are established for *Y. lipolytica* (Larroude et al. 2019; Holkenbrink et al. 2018). Genome-scale models (GEMs), in turn, can also be employed within DBTL cycles for metabolic network optimization (Design) (Chao et al. 2017) and for the integration of cultivation and omics data (Learn) (Czajka et al. 2021; Vijayakumar et al. 2020). The integration of enzyme kinetics (derived from k_cat values) and abundance constraints into GEMs, yielding enzyme-constrained GEMs (ecGEMs), improves their predictive abilities. This integration enables the description of phenotypes previously considered unfeasible (Sánchez et al. 2017). These enhanced predictive abilities also make ecGEMs powerful tools for guiding metabolic engineering efforts. For example, an ecGEM of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* was employed to achieve a 70-fold increase in heme production (Ishchuk et al. 2022).

To date, six original *Y. lipolytica* GEM (Loira et al. 2012; Kavšček et al. 2015; Kerkhoven et al. 2016; Wei et al. 2017; Mishra et al. 2018; Y. Guo et al. 2022) and one ecGEM (Domenzain et al. 2022) reconstructions have been published (excluding updates). However, the latter does not incorporate the latest features of ecGEM reconstruction pipelines, such as GECKO 3 (Chen et al. 2024), which allows for the prediction of k_cat values through deep learning and further improves ecGEM predictions (Li et al. 2022). Moreover, to our knowledge, the existing *Y. lipolytica* ecGEM has not yet been used for metabolic engineering efforts.

The goal of this work was to reconstruct an updated ecGEM of *Y. lipolytica* with expanded deep-learning k_cat constraints. This refined model was then applied to analyze strains engineered to produce industrially relevant molecules, namely lipids and carotenoids. The newly generated ecGEM, when constrained with bioreactor flux and proteomics data, provided additional insights into the metabolism of these strains, complementing a more traditional proteomics approach. Furthermore, these models were used to predict additional targets for metabolic engineering. This predictive application represented the start of a second round of a DBTL cycle. The outputs of these predictions, which appeared to be growth-phase-specific, enabled the rational conceptualization of subsequent steps for improving the genotype of these strains, thereby endorsing the value of ecGEMs as a tool for DBTL cycles.

### Enhancing the heat tolerance of homolactic Escherichia coli through reverse metabolic engineering

Gilberto Pérez-Morales, Josue Vara-Coapango, Enrique Merino, Miguel A. Cevallos, Guillermo Gosset, Alfredo Martinez
*Applied Microbiology and Biotechnology* (2026) 110:26

**Introduction**

Temperature is a critical abiotic factor that influences the survival of living organisms (Haynes 1964). In high-temperature bioprocesses, microbial thermotolerance is essential for producing biotechnologically valuable compounds (Matsushita et al. 2016; Mehta et al. 2016). Although thermophilic microorganisms capable of growing at elevated temperatures are available, their application is limited by complex growth requirements and the scarcity of molecular tools for metabolic engineering (Vavitsas et al. 2022).

*Escherichia coli* remains a preferred platform for biotechnological production due to its well-characterized genetics (Yang et al. 2021), rapid growth, simple nutritional requirements, and a vast repertoire of molecular tools. However, its growth is restricted to mesophilic conditions (19 to 42 °C), with optimal growth occurring at 37 °C (Lenski and Bennett 1993), which limits its use in high-temperature processes.

Enhancing *E. coli* thermotolerance requires shifting its upper thermal limit. Reverse engineering, as outlined by Bailey et al. (2002), involves a three-step process: (i) defining a desired phenotype, (ii) identifying the genetic or environmental determinants of that phenotype, and (iii) transferring them through directed manipulation to endow that phenotype on another strain (Bailey et al. 2002). In this context, defining a thermotolerant *E. coli* phenotype requires stabilizing molecular and cellular processes at supraoptimal temperatures, balancing degeneration with the maintenance of critical functions (Caspeta et al. 2009).

Although short-term acclimatization through the heat shock stress response enables survival under transient stress, expanding *E. coli*'s thermal niche for industrial applications requires stable genetic modifications. Stable genetic changes are essential for allowing *E. coli* to thrive at high temperatures over extended periods. Understanding the molecular and cellular processes that govern thermotolerance, identifying the precise genetic components, their functions, and interactions that confer this trait presents a significant challenge. Direct engineering of thermotolerance is complex due to the incomplete understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms. However, identifying barriers to growth at supraoptimal and lethal temperatures can guide the discovery of functional targets for genetic intervention.

In this study, we developed a reverse metabolic engineering approach to generate thermotolerant strains. To develop an *E. coli* strain for lactic acid production at high temperatures, we used a metabolically engineered d-homolactic strain for thermal adaptive laboratory evolution and to study in depth the effect of mutations acquired during the adaptation process. To achieve this, physiological analysis of thermally adapted strains enabled the identification of a phenotype capable of maintaining homolactic production at 47 °C in mineral medium supplemented with hydrolyzed protein. Comparative genomics of two adapted strains revealed convergent mutations in genes *rpoB*, *metJ*, and *lrp*, which were subsequently validated through genome editing. Single-point mutations in *rpoB* or *metJ* conferred thermotolerance to the parental strain at 45 °C; however, combinatorial analysis revealed only partial additive effects. These findings could help guide the rational design of robust, thermotolerant microbial cell factories for the production of other commodities. In addition, we expect that this information will contribute to understanding thermal adaptation at the molecular level. Finally, we anticipate that these findings will support future work aimed at improving tolerance to acids and inhibitory compounds, enabling optimal application in SSF processes.
