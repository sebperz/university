Article 1. Metabolic engineering of Yarrowia lipolytica targeting bottlenecks to boost D-Pantothenic acid biosynthesis

Objective

To build a Yarrowia lipolytica cell factory that produces D-pantothenic acid (DPA, vitamin B5) from simple carbon sources by identifying and breaking the pathway bottlenecks through systematic metabolic engineering.

Problem addressed

DPA production primarily depends on chemical synthesis, which is environmentally unfriendly because it involves large quantities of toxic gases and wastewater. DPA cell factories have been established in Escherichia coli, Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus megaterium, and Corynebacterium glutamicum. E. coli achieved the highest DPA titer of 148 g/L with β-alanine supplementation and 65.1 g/L without it, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae reached 4.1 g/L in a 1-L bioreactor. There was no report of attempts to engineer Y. lipolytica to synthesize DPA from simple carbon sources.

Methodology

The work started from the engineered Y. lipolytica strain Po1f-HHR, which has a significantly improved homologous recombination efficiency, and applied systematic metabolic engineering. First, the rate-limiting steps were identified and key enzymes were combined for synergistic optimization to enhance the DPA titer. Then, strongly competitive pathways were weakened to redirect the metabolic flux for further enhancement of DPA synthesis. Finally, multiple cofactor supplies were enhanced to improve the efficiency of the entire DPA synthesis pathway.

Results

The combined strategies achieved a DPA titer of 192 mg/L in shake-flask cultures. The best strain, DPA34, reached a DPA titer of 2.18 g/L through fed-batch fermentation in a 5-L bioreactor, representing the first report of DPA production in the Y. lipolytica cell factory.

Comparison with other methodologies

The final 2.18 g/L remains below the 148 g/L and 65.1 g/L reached in E. coli (with and without β-alanine supplementation) and below the 4.1 g/L of S. cerevisiae, while chemical synthesis is still the main production route despite being environmentally unfriendly. The work is the first DPA production reported in Y. lipolytica.

Article 2. Current status, trend and progress of stem cells in tracheal tissue engineering research: an overview and perspectives

Objective

To review the status, trends, and hotspots of stem cell research in tracheal tissue engineering (TTE) through bibliometric analysis, and to summarize the stem cell sources, the construction strategies, and the field's challenges.

Problem addressed

Clinical reconstruction of long tracheal defects (50% or greater in adults, 30% or more in children), caused by tumor invasion, tracheal trauma, tracheomalacia, or infection, remains difficult, and a replacement is needed with suitable biomechanical properties, excellent biocompatibility, no immunogenicity, and no inflammatory response.

Methodology

A comprehensive overview based on bibliometric analysis, which provides qualitative and quantitative analysis of the impact of publications and can reveal research status, trends, and emerging hotspots through dynamic spatial and temporal analysis, as well as the authors, institutions, and countries and their cooperative relationships.

Results

The review investigates the research trend, status, and current hotspots of stem cell research in TTE and uncovers the major influential countries, institutions, and authors. It summarizes the current stem cell sources in TTE research, which include mesenchymal stem cells, pluripotent stem cells, and stem cell-derived exosomes, explores methods for constructing tissue-engineered tracheae based on stem cells, and discusses the challenges confronting the field.

Comparison with other methodologies

The review relies on bibliometric analysis rather than experiments. It notes that tissue engineering has shown positive results in trachea reconstruction, nerve regeneration, vascular reconstruction, cartilage repair, and bone reconstruction, and that stem cells are advantageous because of their multifaceted differentiation potential, immunomodulatory properties, and paracrine features.

Article 3. Enzyme-constrained genome-scale model of Yarrowia lipolytica predicts growth-phase specific metabolic engineering targets

Objective

To reconstruct an updated enzyme-constrained genome-scale model (ecGEM) of Y. lipolytica with expanded deep-learning k_cat constraints, and to use it to analyze strains engineered to produce industrially relevant molecules, namely lipids and carotenoids, and to predict additional metabolic engineering targets.

Problem addressed

Genome-scale models (GEMs) gain predictive power when enzyme kinetics (derived from k_cat values) and abundance constraints are integrated, yielding ecGEMs that can describe phenotypes previously considered unfeasible. Six original Y. lipolytica GEMs and one ecGEM (Domenzain et al., 2022) had been published, but the latter did not incorporate the latest features of ecGEM reconstruction pipelines, such as GECKO 3, and had not yet been used for metabolic engineering.

Methodology

An updated ecGEM of Y. lipolytica was reconstructed with expanded deep-learning k_cat constraints. The refined model was constrained with bioreactor flux and proteomics data and applied to strains engineered to produce lipids and carotenoids.

Results

The new ecGEM provided additional insights into the metabolism of the analyzed strains, complementing a more traditional proteomics approach, and was used to predict additional metabolic engineering targets. The predicted targets appeared to be growth-phase-specific, enabling the rational conceptualization of subsequent steps for improving the strains.

Comparison with other methodologies

The ecGEM improves predictive ability over standard GEMs by integrating enzymatic constraints. As an example of the power of ecGEMs, an ecGEM of Saccharomyces cerevisiae was employed to achieve a 70-fold increase in heme production. The new model also improves on the existing Y. lipolytica ecGEM by incorporating GECKO 3 and deep-learning k_cat predictions.

Article 4. Enhancing the heat tolerance of homolactic Escherichia coli through reverse metabolic engineering

Objective

To develop a thermotolerant D-homolactic Escherichia coli strain through reverse metabolic engineering, using thermal adaptive laboratory evolution to identify and validate the mutations behind thermotolerance.

Problem addressed

Temperature is a critical abiotic factor, and microbial thermotolerance is essential for high-temperature bioprocesses. Thermophilic microorganisms are available but limited by complex growth requirements and the scarcity of molecular tools for metabolic engineering. E. coli is a preferred platform because of its well-characterized genetics, rapid growth, simple nutritional requirements, and vast repertoire of molecular tools, but its growth is restricted to mesophilic conditions (19 to 42 °C), with optimal growth at 37 °C. Short-term acclimatization through the heat shock response is only transient, so stable genetic modifications are needed for processes such as simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF).

Methodology

A reverse metabolic engineering approach, following the three-step framework of Bailey et al. (2002): defining the desired phenotype, identifying its genetic or environmental determinants, and transferring them by directed manipulation. A metabolically engineered D-homolactic strain was used for thermal adaptive laboratory evolution, followed by physiological analysis, comparative genomics of two adapted strains, and validation of the mutations through genome editing.

Results

Physiological analysis identified a phenotype capable of maintaining homolactic production at 47 °C in mineral medium supplemented with hydrolyzed protein. Comparative genomics of the two adapted strains revealed convergent mutations in the genes rpoB, metJ, and lrp. Single-point mutations in rpoB or metJ conferred thermotolerance to the parental strain at 45 °C, whereas combinatorial analysis revealed only partial additive effects.

Comparison with other methodologies

The reverse engineering approach contrasts with direct engineering of thermotolerance, which is complex because the underlying molecular mechanisms are incompletely understood, and with heat shock acclimatization, which provides only transient resistance. Thermophilic microorganisms are an alternative for high-temperature processes but are limited by complex growth requirements and scarce molecular tools. The findings are expected to support robust, thermotolerant microbial cell factories and to help improve tolerance to acids and inhibitory compounds for optimal application in SSF processes.
